## 6.1 Data {#section-6-1}

**Time:** about 20 minutes (13 minutes reading, 7 minutes exercises).


### Data and information, again

In Week 1 you met the difference:

- **Data** is raw facts: `38.6`, `2026-11-20`, `Olivia`.
- **Information** is data that has been processed so it means something: *"Olivia had a fever on 20 November."*

In Week 2 you saw that, inside the computer, all data is binary. This week is about data at a larger scale: how organisations collect it, store it, look after it and, eventually, delete it. Data analysts turn data into information; cyber security analysts protect it.

### Structured, semi-structured and unstructured data

| Kind | What it looks like | Examples | Easy to search and count? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structured** | Rows and columns, where every row has the same fields | A spreadsheet of appointments; a bank's transaction table; a CSV file | Yes |
| **Semi-structured** | Labelled fields, but not a fixed table; records can differ | JSON data from a website; an email (labelled To, From, Date and Subject, then free text); the Week 3 `server.log` | Partly |
| **Unstructured** | No fixed fields | Photos, X-ray images, videos, voice recordings, scanned letters, free-text notes | No, not without extra tools |

Here is the same appointment stored three ways:

**Structured (CSV, a table):**

```
date,time,client,service
2026-11-24,09:30,Sam Taylor,Initial consultation
```

**Semi-structured (JSON, labelled fields):**

```json
{"date": "2026-11-24", "time": "09:30",
 "client": {"name": "Sam Taylor"},
 "service": "Initial consultation"}
```

**Unstructured (a note):**

:::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::: callout

Sam rang this morning, can he come in Tuesday at half nine for his first appointment?

::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::::

A computer can sort or count the first two easily. To use the third, a person (or an AI tool) must read it first.

Most of the world's data is unstructured. A data scientist spends much of their time turning unstructured and semi-structured data into structured tables that can be analysed.

### Where data is stored

You do not need to know how to build these, only what they are:

- **Files and folders** on a device or a shared drive (Week 3). Simple, but hard to share safely and easy to lose.
- **Databases:** software designed to store large amounts of structured data, let many people use it at once, and find any record quickly. Behind almost every app, from banking to booking systems, is a database.
- **Data centres:** buildings full of servers, storage and network equipment, with backup power and cooling. When you use "the cloud", your data is in someone's data centre (Section 6.2).

### The data lifecycle

Data has a life, from the moment it is collected to the moment it is deleted. Each stage brings its own risks.

| Stage | What happens | Example at a physio clinic | A question to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Collect** | Data is gathered | A new client fills in a form | Do we need every field we ask for? |
| 2. **Store** | It is saved somewhere | Entered into the booking system | Is it secure and backed up? |
| 3. **Use** | It is processed to do a job | Booking appointments, writing treatment notes | Is it used only for the reason it was collected? |
| 4. **Share** | It is sent to others | A report sent to the client's GP | Who receives it, and is it sent safely? |
| 5. **Archive** | It is kept, but no longer in daily use | Records of past clients | How long must we keep it? |
| 6. **Delete** | It is destroyed securely | Old records destroyed after the legal period | Is it really gone, including backups and old computers? |

Good practice is to **collect less** (less data means less to lose) and to **delete what you no longer need**.

### Data quality

Decisions are only as good as the data behind them. Analysts often say "garbage in, garbage out". Good-quality data is:

| Quality | Meaning | Problem example |
|---|---|---|
| **Accurate** | Correct | A phone number with two digits swapped |
| **Complete** | Nothing important missing | Half the clients have no email address |
| **Consistent** | The same thing is written the same way everywhere | `NSW`, `N.S.W.` and `New South Wales` in one column |
| **Timely** | Up to date | A client's old address after they moved |
| **Unique** | No duplicates | Sam Taylor entered twice, so they get two reminder texts |
| **Valid** | In the right format and range | A date of birth of `31/02/1990`, or an age of `250` |

Checking data as it is entered is called **validation**. An algorithm like the ones you designed in Week 4 can do it, for example by rejecting an impossible date.

### Personal and sensitive information

Some data is about people, and the law treats it specially (Section 6.3).

- **Personal information** is information about an identified person, or one who could reasonably be identified. Names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, photos, and even a combination of facts that points to one person.
- **Sensitive information** is a special kind of personal information that could cause serious harm if misused. It includes **health information**, racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, sexual orientation, criminal record, and biometric data such as fingerprints.

A physio clinic holds a great deal of health information, so it must take particular care.

## 6.2 The cloud {#section-6-2}

**Time:** about 20 minutes (13 minutes reading, 7 minutes exercises).


### What the cloud is

**Cloud computing** means using computing resources (processing, storage, software) that run in someone else's data centre, over the internet, and paying for what you use. There is no actual cloud: "the cloud" is simply **other people's computers**, in large data centres.

Before the cloud, a business that needed a server had to buy one, find a room for it, keep it cool, update it and replace it every few years. With the cloud, it rents what it needs, when it needs it.

You already use the cloud: web email, online photo storage, streaming services, Google Docs and Microsoft 365, and every browser tool in this course (JSLinux, CyberChef and diagrams.net are web pages that run in your browser, delivered from servers).

### Service models: IaaS, PaaS and SaaS

The three main **service models** differ in how much the provider manages for you.

| Model | What you rent | You manage | Provider manages | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IaaS** (infrastructure as a service) | Virtual servers, storage and networks | The operating system, software and data | The physical hardware, building, power and network | Renting a virtual Linux server from Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud |
| **PaaS** (platform as a service) | A ready-made platform to run your own programs | Your program and its data | Everything else, including the operating system and updates | A developer uploads a web app, and the platform runs it |
| **SaaS** (software as a service) | Finished software, used in a browser or app | Your data and who has access | Everything else | Gmail, Microsoft 365, Canvas, an online booking system |

A common way to remember it is **pizza as a service**:

- **On-premises** (your own servers): you make the pizza at home from scratch.
- **IaaS:** you buy a take-and-bake pizza; you still bake it, and serve it at your table.
- **PaaS:** it is delivered hot; you only provide the table and drinks.
- **SaaS:** you eat at the restaurant; you just turn up.

Most small businesses mainly use **SaaS**.

### Deployment models: public, private and hybrid

| Model | What it is | Typical user |
|---|---|---|
| **Public cloud** | The provider's data centres, shared by many customers (each kept separate) | Most businesses and individuals |
| **Private cloud** | Cloud-style computing used by one organisation only, in its own data centre or a dedicated one | Banks, large government agencies |
| **Hybrid cloud** | A mix: some systems in a private cloud or on site, some in the public cloud | An organisation that keeps its most sensitive data close but uses the public cloud for everything else |

### Benefits and risks

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| **No hardware to buy**, and pay only for what you use | **Ongoing cost:** subscriptions add up, and prices can rise |
| **Scale up or down** quickly | **Needs the internet:** if the connection fails, so does access |
| **Access from anywhere**, on any device | **Access from anywhere** for attackers too, if passwords are weak and MFA is off |
| **The provider handles** hardware, power, cooling and often backups and updates | **Less control:** you depend on the provider's security and on it staying in business |
| **Often more secure** than a small business could manage alone | **Where is the data?** It may be stored overseas (below) |
| Easy **sharing and collaboration** | **Misconfiguration:** a folder accidentally shared as "anyone with the link" is a data breach |

### Data sovereignty: where is my data?

Data in the cloud is stored in a real data centre in a real country. **Data sovereignty** is the idea that data is subject to the laws of the country where it is stored.

Why it matters:

- Another country's laws may let its government request access to data stored there.
- Under the Australian Privacy Principles (Section 6.3), an Australian business that sends personal information overseas must take reasonable steps to make sure it is protected (APP 8).
- Many organisations, including Australian government agencies and health services, prefer or require data to stay in Australia.

The large cloud providers have **regions** in Australia (for example Sydney and Melbourne), so customers can choose to keep data onshore. When choosing a cloud service, ask: *"In which country is our data stored, and in which country are its backups?"*

### The shared responsibility model

Moving to the cloud does not hand over all responsibility. The provider is responsible for the security **of** the cloud (buildings, hardware, its own systems). The customer is always responsible for security **in** the cloud:

- who has accounts, and with what permissions (least privilege, Week 3)
- strong passwords and MFA (Week 5)
- what is shared, and with whom
- the data itself

Many cloud breaches are not caused by the provider being hacked, but by a customer's mistake, such as a stolen password without MFA or storage left open to the public.

## 6.3 Privacy, ethics and society {#section-6-3}

**Time:** about 30 minutes (20 minutes reading, 3 minutes in your browser settings, 7 minutes exercises).


### Digital footprints

Your **digital footprint** is the trail of data you leave behind when you use digital services.

| Kind | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Active footprint** | Data you choose to share | Posts, comments, photos, reviews, online forms, your profile |
| **Passive footprint** | Data collected about you, often without you noticing | your user name and login times in a server log (Week 3), cookies, location history, the websites and apps you use, how long you watch a video, the dozens of requests behind one news page (Week 5) |

Footprints matter because:

- they are **hard to delete**: copies, screenshots and archives last;
- they are **combined**: many small facts together can identify you, or guess your habits, income or health;
- they are **used**: by employers checking applicants, by advertisers, and by scammers making a phishing message convincing (Week 5).

**Cookies** are small files a website saves in your browser. **First-party** cookies come from the site you are visiting and do useful things, such as keeping you logged in. **Third-party** cookies come from other companies, often advertisers, and can follow you from site to site.

### Try it: your browser's privacy settings

About 3 minutes. **Look only; do not write down anything from your own history or accounts.** On a work laptop, some settings may be locked by IT; that is fine.

1. Open your browser's settings:
   - **Chrome:** Settings → **Privacy and security** → **Third-party cookies**, then **Site settings**.
   - **Edge:** Settings → **Privacy, search, and services** → **Cookies and site permissions**.
   - **Safari (Mac):** Safari → Settings → **Privacy**.
2. Find whether **third-party cookies** are allowed or blocked.
3. In **site permissions**, see which kinds of permission websites can ask for: location, camera, microphone, notifications.
4. Think: which of these would you want a site to ask you about first?

### Australian privacy law

#### The Privacy Act 1988

The **Privacy Act 1988** (Cth) is Australia's main privacy law. It is overseen by the **Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC)**, https://www.oaic.gov.au/.

It covers Australian Government agencies, and businesses with an annual turnover of **more than $3 million**. Most smaller businesses are not covered, **but some are, regardless of turnover**, including any **health service provider** and any business that trades in personal information (OAIC n.d.a). So a small physio clinic **is** covered.

#### The Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)

The Act contains **13 Australian Privacy Principles**. You do not need to memorise them all. These are the ones you will use most:

| APP | In plain English |
|---|---|
| **APP 1** Open and transparent management | Have a clear, up-to-date privacy policy that says what you collect and why. |
| **APP 3** Collection | Collect only what you need for your job. Collecting **sensitive** information (such as health information) usually needs the person's consent. |
| **APP 5** Notification | Tell people, when you collect their information, who you are, why you are collecting it and who it may be given to. |
| **APP 6** Use or disclosure | Use information only for the reason it was collected, unless an exception applies (for example, the person agrees). |
| **APP 8** Cross-border disclosure | Before sending personal information overseas (including to an overseas cloud service), take reasonable steps to make sure it is protected. |
| **APP 10** Quality | Keep information accurate, up to date and complete (Section 6.1). |
| **APP 11** Security | Protect information from misuse, loss and unauthorised access, and destroy or de-identify it when it is no longer needed. |
| **APP 12** and **APP 13** Access and correction | People can ask to see the information held about them, and to have it corrected. |

The full list is in the OAIC's quick reference: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-quick-reference

#### The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme

A **data breach** happens when personal information is lost, or accessed or disclosed without permission: a stolen laptop, a hacked database, or an email sent to the wrong person.

Under the **NDB scheme**, an organisation covered by the Privacy Act must notify **the affected people** and **the OAIC** when a data breach is **likely to result in serious harm** to someone (OAIC n.d.b). If it suspects a breach but is not sure, it must assess it quickly (within 30 days). The notice to people must tell them what steps to take, for example changing passwords or watching for scams.

The scheme links this week to Week 5: a phishing email that steals a receptionist's password could become a notifiable data breach.

### The Essential Eight

The **Essential Eight** is a set of eight strategies from the **Australian Signals Directorate (ASD)** to protect organisations from common cyber attacks (ASD 2023). It is widely used in Australia, and you will meet it again in cyber security study.

| Strategy | In plain English | You met it in |
|---|---|---|
| **Patch applications** | Install updates for apps (browsers, Office, PDF readers) quickly | Week 3 |
| **Patch operating systems** | Install operating system updates quickly, and replace systems that no longer get updates | Week 3 |
| **Multi-factor authentication** | Require a second factor, not just a password | Week 5 |
| **Restrict administrative privileges** | Only a few people have admin accounts, and they use them only for admin tasks | Week 3 (least privilege) |
| **Application control** | Only approved programs are allowed to run | New (it builds on software in Week 3) |
| **Restrict Microsoft Office macros** | Block macros (small programs inside Office files), a common way malware arrives in attachments | New (it builds on unexpected attachments in Week 5) |
| **User application hardening** | Turn off risky features in browsers and apps that are not needed | New |
| **Regular backups** | Back up important data, test that it can be restored, and keep a copy that ransomware cannot reach | Week 5 |

Organisations measure themselves against a **maturity model** with levels from **zero** (not done) to **three** (done thoroughly). A small business would usually aim for level one first.

Full details: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/essential-eight-explained

### Artificial intelligence: bias and responsible use

In video 3 you saw that **machine learning** systems learn patterns from **training data**, rather than following rules a person wrote. This is powerful, but:

- **Bias in, bias out.** If the training data is unbalanced or reflects past unfairness, the system repeats it. For example, a CV-screening tool trained on a company's past hires, who were mostly men, may learn to rank women lower. A face-recognition system trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces may make more mistakes with darker-skinned faces. This is data quality (Section 6.1) with real consequences for people.
- **It is hard to explain.** Many AI systems cannot easily say *why* they made a decision, which makes errors hard to spot and challenge.

**Generative AI** (tools that write text, code or images, such as chatbots) brings its own cautions:

1. **Check everything.** It can produce confident, fluent answers that are wrong, including made-up facts and references.
2. **Protect data.** Never paste personal, client or confidential work information into a public AI tool; it may be stored and used outside your control (a possible breach of APP 6 or APP 11).
3. **Be honest about use.** Follow ADCI's and your employer's rules, and say when you used AI.
4. **You are responsible** for anything you submit or act on, not the tool.

Australia has eight voluntary **AI Ethics Principles**, including fairness, privacy protection, transparency, contestability (people can challenge a decision) and accountability: https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/australias-ai-ethics-principles

### Computing and society

Computing changes much more than technology.

- **Work and automation.** Computers take over routine tasks (data entry, simple checking, some customer service), and many jobs change rather than disappear. New jobs appear, including the cyber security and data roles this course prepares you for. Digital skills matter in almost every job.
- **Education.** Online learning, like this course, lets working adults study anywhere and at any time. It also depends on having a device, a reliable connection and the skills to use them.
- **The digital divide.** Not everyone has the same access, affordability or digital skills. In Australia, people in remote areas, older people, people on low incomes and some First Nations communities are more likely to be left behind. As more services (banking, health, government) move online, this gap matters more. The **Australian Digital Inclusion Index** measures it: https://digitalinclusionindex.org.au/
- **The environment.** Data centres need large amounts of electricity and water for cooling. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about **1.5%** of the world's electricity in 2024, and that this could more than double by 2030, driven partly by AI (IEA 2025). Old devices become **e-waste**, which contains toxic materials and valuable metals; recycling and using devices for longer both help.
- **Online safety and wellbeing.** Cyberbullying, image-based abuse and scams cause real harm. In Australia, the **eSafety Commissioner** (https://www.esafety.gov.au/) helps people report serious online abuse and get harmful content removed.

3. (a) APP 6 (use for another purpose; APP 7, direct marketing, also applies); (b) APP 11 (security), and it may be a notifiable data breach; (c) APP 12 (access).

## Concepts Bridge: Digital footprints and who owns your data {#week-6-bridge}

**Time:** about 30 minutes (15 minutes reading, 15 minutes activity).

### Why this matters

Every analyst works with data about people. A cyber security analyst protects it; a data analyst finds patterns in it. Both need to know where it comes from, who holds it and what rules apply. The easiest way to understand that is to follow one ordinary person through one ordinary day.

### The big idea: one day of data

**Aisha Khan** (an invented person) works in an office in Parramatta. Here is her Tuesday. Notice how much is **passive**: collected without her typing anything.

| Time | What Aisha does | Data created | Who collects it | Active or passive | Personal? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6:30 am | Her smartwatch records her sleep and heart rate | Sleep times, heart rate | The watch maker's cloud service | Passive | Yes, and **health** information, so sensitive |
| 7:40 am | Taps on to the train with her travel card | Station, time, card number | The transport operator | Passive | Yes, if the card is registered in her name |
| 7:45 am | Reads a news site on her phone | Pages read, time spent, device type, IP address | The news site, plus advertising and analytics companies (third-party cookies) | Passive | Often, when combined |
| 8:55 am | Logs in to her work computer | User name, login time, computer name | Her employer's server log (like Week 3) | Passive | Yes |
| 12:30 pm | Buys lunch with her phone | Amount, place, time | Her bank, the payment network and the café | Passive | Yes |
| 12:35 pm | Leaves the café a 5-star review | Review text, her profile name and photo | The review website, and anyone who reads it | **Active** | Yes |
| 5:30 pm | Books a physio appointment online for a sore shoulder | Name, phone, email, date of birth, injury | The clinic, through its cloud booking system (SaaS, Section 6.2) | **Active** | Yes, and **health** information |
| 7:00 pm | Asks a smart speaker to play music | A voice recording and the request | The speaker maker | Active (the request) and passive (the recording) | Yes |
| 9:00 pm | Scrolls social media | Every post viewed, liked or paused on | The social media company | Mostly passive | Yes |

In one day, at least **nine organisations** collected data about Aisha. Put together, the data could reveal where she lives and works, her daily routine, her health, her income and her interests.

### So, who owns your data?

The answer is less simple than it sounds:

- In Australian law, you do not "own" your personal information like a piece of property. Instead, the **Privacy Act** gives you **rights**, and places **duties** on the organisations that hold it (Section 6.3). Those duties apply to the larger organisations and to all health service providers, but not to most small businesses.
- **Your rights** include: being told what is collected and why (APP 5), seeing your information (APP 12), having it corrected (APP 13), and complaining to the OAIC.
- **Their duties** include: collecting only what they need (APP 3), using it only for the reason it was collected (APP 6), keeping it secure (APP 11), and telling you about a serious breach (the NDB scheme).
- **What you post** (photos, reviews) is often still yours in copyright terms, but the website's terms of use usually give it a **licence** to use, copy and show it.
- **Data about you** created by a company (your watch data, your viewing history) is held and controlled by that company, under its privacy policy.

The practical lesson: **the organisation holding the data is responsible for protecting it**. That is exactly the job you are preparing for.
