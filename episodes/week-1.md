## 1.1 What is a computer? {#section-1-1}

**Time:** about 40 minutes (25 minutes reading, 15 minutes exercises).


### Data and information

A **computer** is a machine that takes in data, follows instructions to work on it, and gives back a result. Laptops, phones, smart watches, supermarket checkouts and the chip in a modern car are all computers.

Two words come up all the time in computing, and they are not the same:

- **Data** is raw facts with no meaning on their own. For example: `38.6`, `2026-11-20`, `Olivia`.
- **Information** is data that has been processed so it means something to a person. For example: *"Olivia's temperature was 38.6 °C on 20 November, which is a fever."*

A computer's job is to turn data into information. Data scientists and cyber security analysts do the same thing: they take large amounts of data (sales records, network logs) and turn it into information someone can act on.

### The IPOS cycle

Every computer, from a calculator to a data centre, does four jobs. Together they are called the **IPOS cycle**:

| Step | What happens | Example: paying at a self-service checkout |
|---|---|---|
| **Input** | Data goes into the computer. | The scanner reads each barcode. You tap your card. |
| **Processing** | The computer works on the data by following instructions. | It looks up each price, adds them up and checks your payment. |
| **Output** | The computer gives back a result. | The screen shows the total. The printer prints a receipt. |
| **Storage** | The computer keeps data to use later. | The sale is saved so the shop knows what to reorder. |

Storage can happen at any point. A computer may store data as it comes in, while it works on it, or after it has produced a result.

In [Section 1.2](#section-1-2) you will meet the hardware that does each of the four jobs.

### A brief history of computing

You do not need to learn dates by heart. The point of this short history is to see one pattern: **each new technology made computers smaller, faster, cheaper and more reliable**, until they fitted in a pocket. People often group this history into five **generations**, and the Week 1 video by Byte Sized Explainer uses the same five.

**Before electronics.** For centuries people built mechanical aids for calculating, from the abacus to gear-driven calculating machines in the 1600s and 1800s. They could add and multiply, but a person had to operate every step.

#### 1. Vacuum tubes (1940s to mid-1950s)

The first electronic computers used **vacuum tubes**: glass tubes that act as on-off switches. ENIAC, finished in the United States in 1945, used about 18,000 tubes, weighed about 27 tonnes and filled a large room. Australia's first computer, **CSIRAC**, ran its first program in Sydney in 1949. It is now on display at Scienceworks in Melbourne. Tubes used a lot of power, ran hot and often burnt out.

#### 2. Transistors (mid-1950s to mid-1960s)

The **transistor**, invented in 1947, does the same switching job as a tube, but it is much smaller, cooler and more reliable. Computers built from transistors were smaller and cheaper, and businesses and universities started to buy them.

#### 3. Integrated circuits (mid-1960s to early 1970s)

An **integrated circuit**, or **microchip**, puts many transistors onto one small piece of silicon. Computers shrank from the size of a room to the size of a cabinet.

#### 4. Microprocessors and personal computers (1970s onwards)

A **microprocessor** puts a whole processor on one chip. The first one sold commercially, the Intel 4004 (1971), had about 2,300 transistors. Microprocessors made the **personal computer** possible: a computer cheap enough for one person to own. The Apple II (1977) and the IBM PC (1981) brought computers into homes and offices.

#### 5. Today: mobile, cloud and AI

Since the 2000s, computing has moved into our pockets and onto the internet. A modern smartphone chip holds more than 10 billion transistors. **Cloud computing** lets us use computers in distant data centres, and **artificial intelligence** (AI) systems learn patterns from very large amounts of data. You will look at the cloud and AI in Week 6.

#### The pattern: Moore's law

In 1965 Gordon Moore, who later co-founded Intel, noticed that the number of transistors on a chip was doubling at a steady rate. In 1975 he revised the rate to about every two years. This pattern became known as **Moore's law**. It is an observation, not a law of nature, and it has slowed in recent years. For about 50 years, though, it described why computers kept getting faster and cheaper.

## 1.2 Hardware {#section-1-2}

**Time:** about 70 minutes (40 minutes reading, 30 minutes practice and exercises).


### Hardware and software

**Hardware** is the physical parts of a computer: the things you could touch if you opened it up. **Software** is the instructions that tell the hardware what to do. Hardware without software does nothing, and software needs hardware to run on. You will look at software in Week 3.

Here is how the parts in this section match the four jobs from [Section 1.1](#section-1-1):

| IPOS job | Hardware that does it |
|---|---|
| Input | Keyboard, mouse, touchscreen, microphone, camera, scanner |
| Processing | CPU (and the GPU for graphics) |
| Output | Screen, speakers, printer |
| Storage | RAM (short term), SSD or hard drive (long term) |

### The CPU

The **central processing unit (CPU)**, or **processor**, carries out the instructions in a program. It is often called the "brain" of the computer, although it is really a very fast instruction-follower. It has three main parts:

- **Control unit (CU):** directs everything. It fetches the next instruction from memory, works out what it means (decodes it) and tells the other parts what to do.
- **Arithmetic logic unit (ALU):** does the calculations (adding, subtracting) and the logic (comparing two values, such as "is A bigger than B?").
- **Registers:** a few tiny, extremely fast storage spaces inside the CPU. They hold what the CPU is working on right now. For example, the **program counter** holds the memory address of the next instruction.

The CPU repeats the same three steps billions of times a second. This is the **fetch-decode-execute cycle**:

1. **Fetch** the next instruction from memory.
2. **Decode** it: work out what it means.
3. **Execute** it: carry it out, for example by adding two numbers in the ALU.

You will run a program on a model CPU and watch this cycle step by step in Week 4.

Three more words you will see when you look up a computer's CPU:

- **Clock speed** is how many cycles the CPU can run each second, measured in **gigahertz (GHz)**. 1 GHz is one billion cycles a second, so a 3 GHz CPU runs about 3 billion cycles a second.
- **Cores.** A **core** is a complete processing unit. A CPU with 8 cores can work on 8 tasks at the same time. More cores help most when you run many programs at once.
- **Cache** is a small amount of very fast memory on the CPU chip. It keeps copies of data the CPU is likely to need soon, so it does not have to wait for RAM.

A **graphics processing unit (GPU)** is a second kind of processor. It is built to do thousands of simple calculations at once, which suits graphics, video and AI.

### The motherboard

The **motherboard** is the main circuit board. Everything plugs into it or is built into it: the CPU, the RAM, the storage and the ports (USB, HDMI, audio). The wires that carry data between the parts are called the **bus**. The **power supply** turns mains electricity (or the battery's power) into the low voltages the parts need.

### Memory: RAM and ROM

**Primary memory** is memory the CPU can reach directly. There are two kinds.

**RAM (random access memory)** holds the programs and data you are using right now. When you open a spreadsheet, it is copied from storage into RAM so the CPU can work on it quickly.

- RAM is **volatile**: everything in it is lost when the power goes off. That is why you lose unsaved work in a power cut.
- More RAM lets you keep more programs and larger files open at once without slowing down.
- Typical amounts today are 8 to 32 gigabytes (GB).

**ROM (read-only memory)** holds instructions that must survive when the power is off. The most important one is the **firmware** that starts the computer when you switch it on. On a PC this firmware is called **BIOS**, or **UEFI** on newer machines. It checks the hardware and then loads the operating system.

- ROM is **non-volatile**: it keeps its contents without power.
- In the past, ROM could never be changed. Modern computers keep their firmware on a **flash memory** chip, which can be updated. You will still see it called ROM, because its job is the same.

### Secondary storage

**Secondary storage** keeps your files, programs and operating system long term, even with the power off. It is slower than RAM but much larger and cheaper per gigabyte.

| Type | How it stores data | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard disk drive (HDD)** | Magnetism on spinning metal disks, read by a moving arm | Cheap for large amounts. Slower, and can be damaged if dropped. |
| **Solid-state drive (SSD)** | Flash memory chips, with no moving parts | Much faster, quieter and tougher. Standard in most new laptops. |
| **USB flash drive, SD card** | Flash memory | Small and portable. |
| **Optical disc (CD, DVD, Blu-ray)** | Patterns read by a laser | Now rare. |
| **Cloud storage** | Drives in someone else's data centre, reached over the internet | OneDrive, Google Drive, iCloud. More on this in Week 6. |

### Input and output devices

**Input devices** send data into the computer: keyboard, mouse, trackpad, microphone, webcam, scanner, barcode reader, fingerprint reader. **Output devices** send results out to a person: screen, speakers, headphones, printer.

Some devices do both. A **touchscreen** is an input device (it senses your finger) and an output device (it shows the picture). A network card both sends and receives data.

### The memory hierarchy

Why does a computer need registers, cache, RAM and storage, rather than one kind of memory? Because no single kind is fast, large and cheap all at once.

| Level | Speed | Size | Cost per GB | Keeps data without power? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Registers | Fastest | Tiny (bytes) | Highest | No |
| Cache | Very fast | Small (megabytes) | Very high | No |
| RAM | Fast | Medium (gigabytes) | Medium | No |
| SSD | Slower | Large (hundreds of GB to terabytes) | Low | Yes |
| HDD | Slowest of these | Very large (terabytes) | Lowest | Yes |

The higher up the table, the faster and more expensive the memory. The computer keeps what it needs right now near the top and everything else further down.

## Concepts Bridge: Input, process, output, storage {#week-1-bridge}

**Time:** about 30 minutes (10 minutes reading, 20 minutes activity).

### Why this matters

In Section 1.1 you met the four jobs every computer does: **input, processing, output and storage** (IPOS). This bridge shows that the same four jobs describe *any* computer system, however large, and that each job is done by particular hardware.

Thinking in these four steps is a habit you will use all through your degree:

- A **data scientist** asks: what data comes in, what do we do to it, what result do we report, and what do we keep?
- A **cyber security analyst** asks the same questions to find weak points. Where could someone tamper with the input? Where is sensitive data stored, and who can reach it?

In Week 4 you will use the same steps to design algorithms.

### The big idea: one system, four jobs, real hardware

Here are three everyday systems, with the hardware behind each job.

| System | Input (and the hardware) | Processing | Output (and the hardware) | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supermarket self-checkout | Barcodes (scanner), card tap (card reader), weight (scales) | CPU looks up prices, adds the total, checks the payment | Total (touchscreen), receipt (printer), "bag your item" (speaker) | Sale saved to the store's server; prices kept on its drive |
| ATM | Card (card reader), PIN (keypad), choice (touchscreen or buttons) | CPU checks the PIN with the bank and checks the balance | Cash (dispenser), messages (screen), receipt (printer) | Transaction saved by the bank |
| Fitness watch | Heart rate (light sensor), steps (motion sensor), taps (touchscreen) | Watch CPU counts steps and works out heart rate | Numbers (small screen), buzz (vibration motor) | Day's data in the watch's flash memory, then synced to the cloud |

Two things to notice:

1. **Storage happens in more than one place.** The watch keeps data in its own memory, then copies it to the cloud. This matters for security and privacy: every copy of the data is another place it could leak from.
2. **Input is not only a keyboard.** Sensors, scanners and network connections are all inputs. Many systems have no keyboard at all.

### Worked example: taking a photo on your phone

| Step | What happens | Hardware |
|---|---|---|
| Input | You tap the shutter button, and light comes in through the lens. | Touchscreen, camera sensor |
| Processing | The phone turns the light readings into a picture, sharpens it and makes it smaller. | CPU and GPU, using RAM while it works |
| Output | The photo appears on the screen. | Screen |
| Storage | The photo is saved, and often copied to the cloud. | Flash storage, then cloud storage |
